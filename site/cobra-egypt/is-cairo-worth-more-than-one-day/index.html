Is Cairo worth more than one day?

Short answer

Yes. One day in Cairo covers only Giza and a rushed museum. Three days allow the pyramids at opening, the Grand Egyptian Museum (fully open since November 2025), the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir (open at least until 2030), Saqqara and Dahshur, and a slow afternoon in Islamic and Coptic Cairo and Khan el-Khalili.

The one-day mistake

Most group itineraries give Cairo a single day: pyramids in the morning, a museum after lunch, a flight to Luxor that night. The result is a plateau at its busiest hour, a museum seen at a jog, and a city you never actually entered.

Cairo is a 1,000-year-old capital with the two most important museums of Egyptian antiquity, three necropolises within an hour of each other, and the finest medieval Islamic streetscape in the Arab world. It deserves three days. Here is what each one buys you.

Day one: Giza and the Grand Egyptian Museum

The pyramids first, at opening or, where we can arrange it, before the 7am public opening (8am in Ramadan) so the plateau is quiet. Your Egyptologist walks you from the Great Pyramid to the Sphinx along the causeway, not the coach road. Two to three hours.

Then the Grand Egyptian Museum, two kilometres away. It opened fully in November 2025 and holds the complete Tutankhamun collection, more than 5,000 objects shown together for the first time, plus the Khufu solar boat and the grand staircase of royal statuary. As of 2026 it opens daily from 9am to 6pm with later hours on some evenings; a foreign adult ticket is roughly EGP 1,200–1,600 (about USD 24–32), bookable online with a timed slot. Check the museum's official ticketing site before you go.

Allow four hours. The Tutankhamun galleries alone need 90 minutes. Where available, we arrange after-hours entry for private groups, which turns the museum into a different place.

Day two: Saqqara, Dahshur and Memphis

Forty minutes south of Giza, Saqqara is the necropolis of the Old Kingdom capital. The Step Pyramid of Djoser is the oldest stone building in the world; the Pyramid of Unas holds the earliest Pyramid Texts; the mastabas of Mereruka, Kagemni and Ti have the finest Old Kingdom reliefs anywhere; and the Serapeum is a corridor of granite sarcophagi for the sacred Apis bulls that nobody forgets. Excavation continues and new tombs open to visitors regularly.

Another 25 minutes south, Dahshur has the Bent and Red pyramids of Sneferu, Khufu's father: the two experiments that made Giza possible. Both can be entered. Few people go.

This is the day most serious travellers remember best. See Page 18 for the detail.

Day three: Islamic Cairo, Coptic Cairo and the bazaar

Start in Coptic Cairo, inside the walls of the Roman fortress of Babylon: the Hanging Church, the church of St Sergius and Bacchus, and the Ben Ezra synagogue. Then the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization nearby, where the royal mummies, including Ramses II and Hatshepsut, have been displayed since 2021.

After lunch, Islamic Cairo. The mosque of Ibn Tulun (9th century, the largest and calmest in the city), the Sultan Hassan madrasa beneath the Citadel, and a walk the length of Al-Muizz Street past Mamluk façades, a 14th-century hospital and the surviving city gates. End at Khan el-Khalili as the light goes, when the lanes are cooler and the coppersmiths are still working. A private guide knows which shops are worth entering and which to walk past.

The Egyptian Museum in Tahrir: what is still there in 2026

The old museum has not closed. The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities confirmed in early 2026 that it will keep operating at least until 2030, with a phased renovation planned from 2027 that does not involve full closure.

Tutankhamun has moved to the GEM. What remains is still the deepest collection of Egyptian antiquities in existence: the Narmer Palette, the statue of Djoser, the colossal Amenhotep III and Tiye, the Amarna reliefs, the silver coffin and gold mask of Psusennes I from Tanis, and the tomb of Yuya and Thuya. With Tutankhamun gone the halls are quieter than they have been in a century. If you have a half-day spare, or a particular interest in the Old Kingdom or Amarna, go.

A three-day Cairo at a glance

Day Morning Afternoon Evening
1 Giza plateau at or before opening Grand Egyptian Museum Private dinner facing the pyramids, where arranged
2 Saqqara: Step Pyramid, Unas, Mereruka, Serapeum Dahshur: Red and Bent pyramids; Memphis Rest
3 Coptic Cairo and NMEC Ibn Tulun, Sultan Hassan, Al-Muizz Street Khan el-Khalili at dusk; dinner in the old city or Zamalek

Add a fourth day for the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir and a Nile-side afternoon, or keep it for a slow morning. Cairo is loud. One unscheduled half-day makes the other three better.

Where to stay

Two sensible choices. A hotel on the Giza side with the pyramids in view for the first nights, which puts you ten minutes from the plateau and the GEM; then, if you have three days or more, move to Zamalek or Garden City for the old city and the museums. Or stay in one place and accept forty-minute drives. We will advise based on your dates and the traffic that month.

FAQ

How many days do you need in Cairo? Three. One for Giza and the Grand Egyptian Museum, one for Saqqara and Dahshur, one for Coptic and Islamic Cairo with Khan el-Khalili. A fourth allows the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir and an unhurried half-day.

Is the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir Square still open in 2026? Yes. The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities has said it will operate at least until 2030, with a phased renovation from 2027. Tutankhamun has moved to the GEM; the Narmer Palette, the Tanis treasures and the Amarna galleries remain.

How long do you need at the Grand Egyptian Museum? Three to four hours, with 60–90 minutes in the Tutankhamun galleries. Timed entry is required for foreign visitors; book ahead. Private after-hours visits are arranged where available.
